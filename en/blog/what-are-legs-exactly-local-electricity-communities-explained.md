# What Are LEGs, Exactly? Local Electricity Communities Explained

Since January 1, 2026, something fundamentally new has been available in the Swiss electricity market: private individuals may sell self-generated electricity to their neighbors over the public grid. The framework for this is called a local electricity community, or LEG for short, and originates from the Electricity Supply Act, which was revised as part of the Energy Mantle Decree (the proposal approved by voters in June 2024 with 68.7 percent support). This year, the first LEGs have launched, including in our region, and with the first acceptance confirmations come the questions: What exactly is this? And what does this “20 percent discount on grid usage” mean?

## The principle: local electricity trading over the public grid

Until now, the rule was: Anyone with surplus solar power fed it into the grid and received a feed-in tariff from the grid operator. Anyone who did not generate electricity bought it from the utility. Direct sales to a neighbor were only possible if people physically joined together behind the same grid connection (more on that shortly).

An LEG removes this boundary: producers, storage operators, and consumers in the same area join together contractually and trade electricity among themselves at prices they set themselves, over the regular public grid. Physically, electricity still takes the shortest route as always; the LEG is an accounting construct. The smart meter makes it possible: every 15 minutes, it measures who produces and consumes how much. If one member generates a 2 kWh surplus during a 15-minute interval and other members consume at least 2 kWh at the same time, those 2 kWh are considered delivered within the community.

Anything not covered locally during a 15-minute interval continues to come from the standard supply as before. No one is suddenly “connected only to the sun” because of an LEG.

## The discount: 20 or 40 percent off the grid usage per-kWh rate

The legislator's incentive: electricity traded within the LEG is subject to a reduced grid usage charge. The logic is based on physics: local electricity does not, or barely, use the higher grid levels (high voltage, transmission grid), so it should not pay the full grid tariff either.

The reduction is tiered:

- **40 percent discount** if all participants are connected at the same grid level without transformation; in practical terms, to the same neighborhood grid behind the same transformer station.
- **20 percent discount** once the exchange runs across multiple grid levels, as is the case with any municipality-wide LEG because transformer stations lie between different parts of the municipality.

Important—and this is misunderstood in every discussion: the discount applies **only to the per-kWh rate for grid usage** and **only to kilowatt-hours traded internally within the LEG**. The energy price, ancillary services, federal levies, electricity reserve, metering charges, and basic tariffs remain unchanged. For anyone wanting to know what share of the electricity bill this even represents: [We broke down the bill item by item here](/blog/stromrechnung-verstehen-ekz). Whether anything remains at the bottom line is calculated in [the next article](/blog/lohnt-sich-leg-beitritt).

## LEG, ZEV, vZEV: distinguishing the models

The LEG is the third model in a family, and the terms are frequently mixed up:

| Model | Since | Area | Grid usage for internally traded electricity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ZEV (self-consumption association) | 2018 | Behind the same grid connection (building, site), separate metering | none |
| vZEV (virtual ZEV) | 2025 | Neighboring properties at the same connection point, billing via smart meters | none |
| LEG | 2026 | Same municipality and grid area, over the public grid | reduced (minus 20 or 40 percent) |

In short: In a ZEV and vZEV, the participants own the line (or treat it that way for accounting purposes), so grid usage charges do not apply at all. An LEG uses the public grid and pays for it, just less. In return, it can scale across entire villages and cities.

## Requirements for an LEG

For an LEG to be approved, a few basic rules apply:

- **Same municipality, same grid operator:** All participants must be located within the same municipal area and the grid area of the same distribution system operator.
- **Generation capacity:** Together, the LEG's generation facilities must reach at least 5 percent of the total connected load of all participating end consumers. An LEG without significant generation is not contemplated.
- **Smart meters for everyone:** Without 15-minute metering, no allocation is possible. The broad rollouts of recent years are paying off here.
- **Contract and organization:** The community itself defines prices, allocation keys, and administration; the grid operator provides metering data and bills the reduced grid usage charge.

## Organize it yourself or use the grid operator's offering

In practice, there are two paths. The purist route: establish the LEG yourself, negotiate prices among members, and organize billing (a small market of billing service providers is currently emerging for this). This maximizes flexibility and the potential benefit, but requires organizational work.

The convenient route: many grid operators now offer LEGs as a ready-made product, for example under the name “Gemeinsamstrom” in the EKZ service area. EKZ handles formation, participant administration, and billing; energy prices for customers match the standard supply, and local producers receive a fixed seasonal payment. EKZ charges a service fee per LEG kilowatt-hour for this service. The first municipality-wide LEGs of this kind launched in summer 2026, and enrollment for customers is straightforward: sign up, wait for confirmation, done. Nothing changes at the meter, the connection, or the contract with the grid operator.

Convenience comes at a price, and in the grid operator model it is included in the service fee. How much it eats into the statutory discount depends on your own grid tariff; the details, including a sample calculation, are in the [follow-up article](/blog/lohnt-sich-leg-beitritt), and anyone who wants to enter their own figures can use the [LEG price calculator](/tools/leg-rechner).

## Context: what the LEG is intended to achieve

It is easy to mistake the LEG for an investment vehicle and then be disappointed (spoiler: under the grid operator's standard offering, the financial effect for customers who only consume electricity is roughly zero). It is intended as a market instrument: local generation should find a local buyer and a fair price, producers should earn more than the meager feed-in tariff, and the grid should benefit when electricity is consumed where it is generated. Whether this works will depend on participation—and that begins with understanding what you are signing up for.
