# Is it worth joining a LEG? An honest calculation

The confirmation of admission to a local electricity community sounds promising: «20% discount on grid usage costs». Since this summer, we ourselves have been members of a municipality-wide LEG in the EKZ area, and before the first bill arrives, I wanted to know what this discount means in francs. The short answer surprised me: the amount on our electricity bill will change by almost exactly zero francs. And that is not an oversight, but intentional. The longer answer is still worthwhile, because it shows when a LEG does bring in money.

If LEGs are new to you: [Here is how they work.](/blog/lokale-elektrizitaetsgemeinschaft-leg-erklaert) If the electricity bill is new to you: [Here it is explained line by line.](/blog/stromrechnung-verstehen-ekz)

## Three caveats not mentioned in the welcome letter

The 20% discount sounds like 20% lower electricity costs. In fact, it applies:

1. **Only to the energy-based price for grid usage.** Not to energy, charges, metering or the basic tariff. Under the EKZ grid tariff 400F, this is 7.50 centimes/kWh out of total costs of around 22 centimes/kWh.
2. **Only to the LEG share of consumption.** In other words, to the kilowatt-hours covered, calculated to the quarter-hour, by simultaneous local production. For a household without special load control, this is roughly 20 to 40% of annual consumption, naturally little at night and in winter.
3. **Less the provider's costs.** Anyone who does not organise the LEG themselves, but uses the grid operator's convenience offer, pays a service fee for each LEG kilowatt-hour.

And everything hinges on point 3.

## The calculation using EKZ's «Gemeinsamstrom» offer

EKZ charges a service fee of 1.50 centimes/kWh (excl. VAT) for its LEG product on every LEG kilowatt-hour. The 20% discount under grid tariff 400F amounts to: 7.50 × 0.20 = **1.50 centimes/kWh**.

That is no coincidence; it is product design. Per LEG kilowatt-hour, the calculation looks like this (all figures excl. VAT, EKZ tariffs 2026, summer and winter quarter respectively):

| per kWh | Standard supply | as LEG consumption |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy | 9.00 / 13.30 | 9.00 / 13.30 (standard supply price) |
| Grid usage 400F | 7.50 | 6.00 (minus 20%) |
| LEG service fee | none | 1.50 |
| SDL and charges | 3.19 | 3.19 |
| **Total** | **19.69 / 23.99** | **19.69 / 23.99** |

The discount and the fee cancel each other out exactly. EKZ itself writes that the offer is an «upgrade to local solar power at no extra cost compared with standard supply», and that is precisely what it means: cost-neutral, not cost-reducing. Those on the standard tariff 400ST (7.95 centimes/kWh) gain a calculated 0.09 centimes per LEG kWh; those on the heat-pump tariff 400WP (6.45 centimes/kWh) lose 0.21 centimes. In francs: with annual consumption of 10,000 kWh and an optimistic LEG share of 30%, we are talking about plus or minus a few francs **per year**. That is rounding noise, not a return.

That is also what it looks like for us: the same bill amount as before, only the bill gets new line items (reduced grid usage plus service fee for the LEG share), and in accounting terms part of the electricity consumed now comes from roofs in the municipality rather than from the general supply mix.

## When a LEG can still bring financial benefits

Breaking even applies to one specific, albeit common, case: pure consumer, municipality-wide LEG (20% level), grid operator convenience offer. There are three ways out:

**1. The 40% level.** If all participants are connected to the same transformer station, the double discount applies: 40% of 7.50 centimes is 3.00 centimes, less the 1.50-centime service fee leaves **1.50 centimes per LEG kWh**. At 3,000 LEG kWh per year, that is around 49 francs (incl. VAT). Still not a fortune, but a genuine discount. Neighbourhood LEGs are therefore financially more attractive than municipality-wide LEGs.

**2. Organise it yourself.** The service fee is the price of doing nothing. A self-managed LEG keeps the full discount and sets its energy prices internally; in return, it needs someone to handle contracts, allocation keys and billing. From a certain size and with an affordable billing service provider, this can work out, but hardly for individual households.

**3. Own production.** The real winner from the LEG is the solar installation. EKZ pays LEG producers with up to 30 kWp 11.97 centimes/kWh seasonally (winter quarters) and 9.00 centimes/kWh (summer quarters, both excl. VAT). By contrast, the standard feed-in remuneration is based on the quarterly reference market price; for installations below 30 kW, the law guarantees only 6.00 centimes/kWh, plus up to 3.00 centimes for guarantees of origin. The comparison for 2026:

| Surplus remuneration, excl. VAT | Winter quarter | Summer quarter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feed-in to EKZ (reference market price, with sale of guarantees of origin, capped) | approx. 11.0 centimes/kWh | approx. 9.0 centimes/kWh |
| Sale to the LEG (installation up to 30 kWp) | 11.97 centimes/kWh | 9.00 centimes/kWh |

In summer, when most surplus is generated, the two routes make almost no difference in 2026; in winter, the LEG is about one centime ahead. The greater effect is strategic: LEG remuneration is linked to the energy component of standard supply and is therefore more predictable than the reference market price, which remains under pressure with every additional solar installation. Anyone still planning their installation has a more reliable sales channel with a LEG than with the spot market.

## Are there reasons to leave again?

Financially, under the grid operator model: no, there is simply nothing to gain and nothing to lose. The EKZ offer includes a monthly right to return without switching fees, and customers remain on standard supply for the remaining electricity anyway. As a member, I would nevertheless review two things every year:

- **The ratio of discount to service fee.** In 2026, it is calibrated to exactly zero. If the grid operator changes the grid tariff or fee asymmetrically for the following year, the break-even result may become a small gain or loss. The tariff sheets for the following year are published at the end of August.
- **Compatibility with tariff changes.** Anyone wanting to switch to a dynamic tariff or another energy product should clarify whether this can be combined with LEG consumption.

## Conclusion

The 20% discount is real money, but with the standard offer it does not end up with the consumer; it goes to the provider, which takes care of the entire organisation in return. For pure consumers, joining a LEG is therefore an idealistic decision at no cost: local solar power in the accounting, better remuneration for producers in the village, and no personal risk. For producers and neighbourhood arrangements with a 40% discount, it also becomes financially attractive.
